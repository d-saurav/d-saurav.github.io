An Appreciation for Classical Music 🎻

Classical music understands things before I do. It doesn’t try to fix grief or talk pain away. It just sits with them, and somehow, in its company, they become easier to carry. It has never made the heaviness disappear. What it does is quieter than that: it makes the heaviness feel less lonely.

And it asks for nothing, except that I listen. No argument, no answer expected. A few minutes of attention, and in return it hands back a feeling I hadn’t found the words for yet.

There are two composers I keep coming back to. They sound nothing alike, but both of them find their way to the same place in me.

Ludovico Einaudi: the weight of small things

Einaudi’s music begins the way a memory does: quietly, with a few notes on the piano that keep coming back. In Nuvole Bianche the same phrase returns again and again, and each time it carries a little more. He never insists or tells you what to feel. He leaves the door open and lets you walk in.

That is where the grief is for me. Not in some big swell, but in the repetition. We keep returning to the same faces and the same unanswered questions, long after we think we have let them go. Einaudi doesn’t try to heal that. He just sits beside you while you remember.

Max Richter: On the Nature of Daylight

This piece feels like it started long before you pressed play. Slow strings rise and fall, each line taking its time, nothing rushed. Richter wrote it for The Blue Notebooks (2004), and it sounds less like one person’s sorrow than many people’s at once, which may be why it keeps finding its way into films about loss, like Arrival.

The title stays with me. Daylight is the most ordinary thing there is, and this music suggests that even it holds grief: we love the light most when we know it is ending. It doesn’t resolve the pain. It shows it to you clearly, and somehow that is enough to let you breathe again.

Where they meet

Einaudi and Richter could hardly be more different. One works with a single piano and a few circling notes; the other with a slow tide of strings. One feels intimate, like a diary; the other vast, like a sky.

But both understand something I am still learning: grief is not the opposite of beauty. It is often where beauty comes from. Somewhere in each of us lies a loss we have never fully spoken aloud, and this music does not ask us to explain it. It recognises it, and quietly keeps us company.

Perhaps that is the real gift of music. It does not tell us how to feel; it reminds us that we already do.
